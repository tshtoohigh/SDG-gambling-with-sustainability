How it works

Rescue, sort, box, spin

No algorithm picks your clothes. Four humans and a set of published odds do.

We buy the overflow

Thrift stores can only hang a fraction of what they receive. We buy the rest by weight — the bales normally sold for export or shredding — from 24 partner shops.

Sorters grade every piece

Each garment is checked for holes, pilling, stains and labels, then graded and tagged. Anything unwearable goes to textile recycling, never into a box.

Boxes are packed to the odds

Your box gets 4 everyday staples plus 1–2 feature slots. The feature slots are filled against the same published probability table the reel shows you.

You open it and spin for real

Washed, steamed, wrapped in paper, with a note card naming every piece and the shop it came from. Doesn't fit? Swap it free.

The reveal reel

Open one before you spend anything

Most mystery-box brands hide their odds. Ours are built into the strip: a 6% outcome occupies exactly 4 of these 64 cells. Open it as many times as you like — it's free and it reserves nothing.

A simulator, not the draw. Opening this costs nothing and reserves nothing. Two things we deliberately don't do: we don't pad the strip with extra rare pieces to make it feel richer, and we don't drift the marker toward the edge of your cell to fake a near miss. Those are the two standard tricks in this format.

Why it matters

The clothes already exist. That's the whole point.

The fashion industry's waste problem isn't a shortage of sustainable new clothing. It's the mountain of perfectly wearable garments already sitting in sorting warehouses, priced too low to be worth hanging on a rail.

US landfills took in 11.3 million tons of textiles in 2018 — about 7.7% of everything landfilled that year, according to the US EPA. The blind box is our answer to the economics: bundling one exciting piece with four solid basics makes the whole bundle worth shipping instead of shredding.
