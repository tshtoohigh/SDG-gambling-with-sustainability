Shop boxes

Our story

It started with a bale nobody wanted

Second Spin exists because of a single depressing afternoon in a sorting warehouse, and a suspicion that the mystery-box format — usually a vehicle for selling people junk — could be pointed at something genuinely useful instead.

The afternoon in question

In early 2024, Nadia was volunteering at a charity shop in Leeds when a delivery arrived that the shop simply couldn't process. Three pallets of donations, maybe a tenth of which would ever reach a hanger. The rest was destined for a baler in the back, sold on at pennies per pound.

She pulled a corduroy jacket out of the pile on the way past. Early seventies, perfect seams, a repaired elbow someone had clearly cared about. It was going to be shredded — not because it was worn out, but because the twenty minutes of staff time needed to price and hang it cost more than it would sell for.

That's the real bottleneck in secondhand clothing. Not supply. Not demand. Labour economics. Handling individual cheap garments doesn't pay, so they get handled in bulk, by weight, to whoever will take them.

Why a blind box, of all things

The obvious fix — list everything online individually — fails for the same reason. Photographing, measuring, describing, storing and shipping one $6 jumper costs more in labour than the jumper earns. Every resale platform ends up cherry-picking the small percentage that's worth the effort and leaving the rest in the bale.

Bundling changes that arithmetic. One photo, one listing, one shipment, six garments. Suddenly the ordinary jumper is economically viable to rescue, as long as something in the parcel makes it exciting enough to buy unseen.

Which is where we had to be careful. Mystery boxes have an ugly reputation, and mostly they've earned it: hidden odds, inflated "retail values", loot-box psychology aimed at people who can least afford it. We wanted the format without that. So we set a constraint at the start that we've never relaxed — publish the odds, draw the reveal to scale, and audit ourselves in public.

It costs us conversions. A visitor who reads "55% chance of an everyday basic" sometimes leaves. We think that visitor leaving is a better outcome than that visitor feeling cheated.

Where we are now

Nine sorters across two benches, 24 partner shops, and just over twelve thousand garments kept in circulation. Still small. Still deliberately unglamorous. The most common thing in any box we send is a decent jumper that somebody was going to throw away, and we're genuinely fine with that being the headline.

How it went

Two years, roughly honestly told

  1. March 2024

    The corduroy jacket

    Nadia buys one bale with her own money to find out what's actually in them. Result: 140 garments, 96 of them wearable, 4 genuinely special.

  2. August 2024

    First 40 boxes, packed on a kitchen table

    Sold to friends and friends-of-friends at cost. The feedback that shaped everything: "I liked it, but I had no idea what my chances were."

  3. January 2025

    We publish the odds table

    Against a fair amount of advice. Conversion rate drops about 8% overnight. Repeat purchase rate roughly doubles over the following quarter.

  4. June 2025

    The reveal reel, built to scale

    Our first version was a prize wheel that inflated the rare slices for visual drama. We rebuilt the whole reveal as a strip generated directly from the probability table, because the first version was, on reflection, a lie with nice animation.

  5. February 2026

    Monthly public odds audit

    We start publishing realised feature-slot rates against advertised rates. One month came in low on Vintage Rare; we credited 61 orders and wrote it up.

  6. Today

    Nine sorters, two benches, 24 shops

    Still buying bales nobody else wants. Still telling you the odds before you pay.

Our constraints

Five things we've promised not to do

These are the commitments we'd have to break to grow faster. Written down here so you can hold us to them.

See how the odds work

  • 1Never hide the odds. Published, dated, versioned, and audited monthly in public.
  • 2Never sell new clothing. Not deadstock, not overruns, not "sustainable basics" as filler.
  • 3No loot-box psychology. No timers, no streaks, no artificial scarcity, and no paid re-roll that can leave you worse off. Our one paid second chance, Trade Up, is a guaranteed upgrade capped at one per slot — if we ever ship a mechanic you can lose money on, we've broken this promise.
  • 4No inflated "retail value" claims. We quote realistic resale bands, not RRPs nobody ever paid.
  • 5Never guess at impact numbers. We publish weight we've measured, and refuse to invent water or carbon figures.
