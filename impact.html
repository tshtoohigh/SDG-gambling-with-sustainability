Shop boxes

Why it matters

We are not short of clothes. We are short of ways to keep wearing them.

Enough wearable clothing already exists. The problem is economic: sorting, photographing and listing a $6 jumper costs more than the jumper earns. So it gets baled, exported or buried. The blind box is our way of making that garment worth handling again.

Follow the garment

What normally happens, and where we interrupt it

1. Donated

Someone drops a bag at a thrift store, assuming it will be sold locally.

2. Triaged

The shop hangs what it has floor space and staff hours for. That's a small fraction.

3. Baled by weight

The rest is compressed and sold by the pound for export, shredding or landfill. This is where we buy.

4. Graded by hand

We open bales on a bench, grade every piece, wash and steam what passes.

5. Worn again

Four staples plus one or two feature pieces, boxed to someone who'll actually wear them.

Why a blind box, specifically?

Because the maths of individual resale is what kills these garments. Photographing, measuring, listing, storing and shipping one $6 jumper costs more in labour than it earns. Bundle it with five others and one exciting find, though, and the whole parcel becomes worth handling — one photo, one listing, one shipment.

The mystery format isn't a gimmick we bolted on. It's the only pricing model we found that makes rescuing ordinary clothing viable, rather than cherry-picking the 2% that's resale-worthy and leaving the rest in the bale.

Receipts

How we count, and what the counter actually measures

The diverted-weight counter

Every box is weighed on a connected scale at the packing bench before it's sealed. The counter is the sum of those weights, minus packaging. It is not an estimate, a model or an industry multiplier.

What it doesn't claim: that all 12,450 lbs would definitely have been landfilled. Some of that weight would have been exported or recycled instead. What we can say precisely is that this weight was bought out of the by-the-pound bale stream and put back into wardrobes.

What we refuse to claim

Plenty of brands would put a "saves 2,700 litres of water!" badge on this page. We won't, and here's the honest reasoning:

  • No per-box water or CO₂ figure. Published conversion factors for garment reuse vary enormously depending on assumptions about what the reused item displaces. Any single number we printed would be a marketing choice dressed as measurement.
  • Shipping has a real footprint. A box driven to your door is not zero-impact. If you have a good local charity shop, shopping there beats us. We'd rather say that than pretend otherwise.
  • Roughly a third of what we buy never makes a box. It fails grading and goes to textile recycling. That's honest yield, not waste we hide.
  • Blind boxes can encourage overbuying. That's the real risk in our model, so we cap subscriptions at one box a month, run no countdown timers, no "limited drop" urgency and no loot-box streak rewards.
  • We are not a recycling solution. We extend the life of clothing that already exists. Buying less, and wearing what you own for longer, beats any box we could send.
